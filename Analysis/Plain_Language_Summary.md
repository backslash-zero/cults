# What this research has found so far — in plain words

A non-technical summary of everything in this analysis folder, what it adds up to, and where it
could go next. Technical detail and all numbers live in the numbered documents
([`00`](00_Available_Data.md)–[`10`](10_Secular_Groups_vs_Sectarian_Drifts.md)), indexed from
[`Results_Draft.md`](Results_Draft.md).

---

## What the research is actually doing

The thesis asks what people mean by the word **"cult"** — and in particular whether the reasons
we give for calling some groups cults would, applied honestly, also cover a lot of ordinary
organizations.

Three very different sources are compared:

1. **The French state.** MIVILUDES, the government's anti-cult mission, publishes an official
   list of **17 warning signs** ("sectarian drifts") — things like psychological subjection,
   deceptive recruitment, difficulty leaving, excessive financial demands.
2. **Academic literature.** 68 books, chapters and articles on cults and new religious
   movements, from which about **5,700 individual statements** were extracted.
3. **Ordinary people.** **28 interviews**, where each person was asked what comes to mind when
   they hear the word "cult."

The method, in one sentence: every sentence, criterion and group name is turned into a list of
numbers ("an embedding") that captures its meaning, so that **things that mean similar things end
up near each other**, and we can then measure distances — which criteria sit near which
statements, what each source's "centre of gravity" is, and what sits near nothing at all.

---

## What we found

### 1. The three sources are not talking about the same thing

Each source's centre of gravity sits in a completely different place:

- **The French state's material** centres on its own institutional language — it mostly repeats
  its own core term, "sectarian deviations."
- **The academic literature** centres on **definitional argument** — not descriptions of groups,
  but debate about what to call them.
- **The interviews** centre on generic conversational speech, and where people do say something
  substantive, they reach for **major established religions** (Christianity, Judaism, Islam,
  Mormonism).

This isn't three emphases on one shared idea. It's three different activities: an agency logging
complaints, scholars arguing over category boundaries, and ordinary people free-associating.

### 2. The literature's single biggest activity is arguing about terminology

Set aside the clusters about specific groups, and the two largest remaining topics in the
academic corpus are literally the words **"new religious movement"** (143 statements) and
**"cult"** (117 statements) — spread across many different books, so it isn't one author's
obsession. By volume, the biggest thing this field does is **debate what to call the thing it
studies**.

Relatedly: the literature's abstract, theoretical discourse has **no specific group anywhere near
it**. Its concrete discourse is extremely well anchored (statements about Heaven's Gate sit almost
perfectly on top of "Heaven's Gate"), but its definitional and evaluative statements float free of
any real example.

### 3. Some official criteria are well-grounded; others are essentially untranslatable

Of the 17 official warning signs, the ones every source recognises are those with a long research
tradition behind them — **radical behaviour change**, **authoritarian and opaque organisation**,
**psychological subjection**, **difficulty leaving**.

The ones nobody outside the French system has vocabulary for come dead last, consistently, in all
three sources: **"importance of legal disputes"** and **"violation of the founding principles of
the Republic."** These are administrative and constitutional categories. That doesn't make them
wrong — it means agreement tracks **whether a shared descriptive tradition exists**, not whether
a criterion is real.

### 4. "Brainwashing" is central because it's *argued about*, not because it's accepted

This is an important warning about reading these results. The method measures **what a corpus
talks about**, which includes **what it argues against**. A heavily-disputed idea can look just as
"central" as an accepted one.

Checked directly: "brainwashing" is the single most central term in the literature's *contested*
statements — a tiny group of just 14 statements, 0.2% of the corpus — while the other 99.4%
centre on plain definitional claims instead. So brainwashing's apparent centrality comes from it
being a live fight, not a settled finding.

### 5. Ordinary people's first thought lands where the literature has the least to say

Each interview's very first free-association answer (the "prototype" — the thing that comes to
mind first, which prototype theory treats as the most revealing) was compared to the literature's
59 topic clusters.

**13 of 25 (52%) land in a region where the literature has no specific group vocabulary at all** —
roughly double the base rate of 25%. The most theoretically privileged answer people give is
*more* likely than ordinary interview chatter to land in the literature's blind spots. Folk
concept and expert concept are not just different in content; they're pointed at different kinds
of object.

### 6. The central hypothesis holds: the criteria don't single out religion

This is the thesis's own central claim — that the basis for calling some groups cults would imply
the same of other social structures. Tested two ways:

- A small hand-picked set of **non-religious** structures already discussed in the literature
  (Amway, Nazism, Maoist thought reform, secular self-improvement seminars).
- A broad list of **169 ordinary non-religious organizations** (charities, unions, hobby clubs,
  campaign groups, professional bodies, sports leagues).

Both score **as close to the official criteria as** — and mostly closer than — a control set of
famous religious cults (Heaven's Gate, Aum Shinrikyo, Jonestown, and so on). Going criterion by
criterion, this holds for **all 17 without exception**. The widest gaps are the most procedural
warning signs — **excessive financial demands** and **deceptive recruitment** — which is exactly
what you'd expect if the hypothesis is right, since exploitative finance and misleading
recruitment are generic organisational problems with nothing religious about them.

### 7. But the concept is anchored precisely where it *doesn't* generalise

The most interesting single result. **Psychological subjection** ("mental destabilisation"):

- is the **most central** of the 17 criteria when you look at the official list on its own terms;
- is the criterion the literature **fights over most** (see finding 4);
- and ranks **dead last of 17** for ordinary organizations — it is the nearest warning sign for
  only 2 of the 169.

So the warning signs generalise almost everywhere **except at the concept's own core**. Whatever
is distinctively "cult" about the official framework is concentrated in a psychological claim that
is simultaneously the most load-bearing, the most disputed, and the least transferable.

### 8. Two honest limits — one of which is itself a finding

**The method measures topic, not conduct.** Comparing an organization's *name* to a criterion's
*wording* picks up shared subject matter, not behaviour. The clearest demonstration: the four
organizations that come out closest to *"great difficulty for a member to leave said group"* are a
mountain-biking group, an Arduino users' group, triathlon clubs and soccer fan clubs — matched on
the word **"group/club"**, nothing more. The single highest match in the entire run is
**"Free the Children" against "indoctrination of children"** — a children's charity.

**Wording alone moves the numbers.** Adding the word **"The"** in front of an organization's name
makes it score measurably *more* cult-like — by more than the size of the real effects being
measured. This forced the retraction of one earlier result (documented openly in
[`08`](08_Secular_Groups_Hypothesis.md) rather than quietly deleted).

Both limits point the same way: these measurements are about **language**, not about what groups
do. That's worth stating plainly — but it doesn't dissolve finding 6, it *relocates* it. The
criteria are written in such ordinary vocabulary (group, children, food, money, recruitment,
leaving) that **their language alone does not exclude perfectly benign organizations.**

### 9. A bonus that cuts against popular intuition

The organizations people most readily call "basically a cult" — the NFL, intense tech companies,
the Olympics — are the **furthest of all 169** from this corpus's cult concept. Meanwhile the
**anti-cult watchdogs** (Southern Poverty Law Center, Anti-Defamation League) and **mental-health
institutions** score near the top.

That makes sense once you see what's being measured: an organization that *writes about*
manipulation and deviance sits right next to the groups accused of practising it. Popular
"that's a cult" talk is about **intensity of belonging**; this concept is about **belief,
ideology and psychological harm**. They're not the same axis.

## What it all means

Put together, a coherent picture:

**"Cult" is not held together by a shared definition. It's held together by a shared argument.**
The literature's main activity is arguing about the term. The state's criteria are partly
grounded in research tradition and partly in French administrative law. Ordinary people use the
word for something else again — big established religions and vague unease.

**The category's boundary work rests on its least settled claim.** The only criterion that really
distinguishes cults from ordinary organizations is psychological subjection — and that's exactly
the claim the field itself has not resolved. Remove it and the remaining 16 warning signs describe
a very large number of normal institutions.

**So the thesis's hypothesis is supported, with an important refinement.** It isn't that the
criteria are sloppy. It's that they are written in the ordinary language of organizational life,
and the one thing that would narrow them to genuine cults is the one thing under dispute.

## Where this could go next

**Quick wins, closing the known gaps:**

1. **Use descriptions, not names.** The biggest single fix. Score each organization against the
   criteria using a paragraph about **what it actually does**, not its name. The source lists
   already contain one-line descriptions that are currently discarded. This turns "the criteria's
   wording doesn't exclude benign groups" into a real test of whether the criteria *apply*.
2. **A clean comparison set.** The current 169 over-represent advocacy, education and health
   bodies. Rerun with deliberately neutral structures — MLMs, fandoms, workplaces, gyms, sports
   clubs, student societies — for an unconfounded version of finding 6.
3. **Fix the remaining wording artifact** by re-running the control groups in the same
   capitalisation as everything else.

**Stronger studies, genuinely new results:**

4. **Compare the French criteria against French-language scholarship.** Right now "legal disputes"
   and "Republic principles" score badly, but the criteria are French and the literature is
   English — so we can't tell whether these categories lack *scholarly* vocabulary or merely
   lack *English* vocabulary. A French academic corpus would separate those two explanations
   cleanly. This is the most important confound left in the whole project.
5. **Turn the artifact into an experiment.** Deliberately include anti-cult organizations,
   watchdog groups and cult researchers in the comparison set. If the people who *study and fight*
   cults score as "cult-like" as cults do, that's a striking, publishable demonstration of what
   these criteria — and this kind of measurement — actually track.
6. **Validate against human judgement.** Give people real descriptions of a sample of ordinary
   organizations and ask them to score each against the 17 criteria. If humans also can't keep the
   criteria away from normal organizations, the hypothesis stops being a geometric claim and
   becomes an empirical one about assessment practice. If humans *can*, that tells you exactly
   what the criteria rely on that the wording doesn't capture.
7. **Ask interviewees for a definition as well as an example.** The current protocol elicits a
   first association. Adding "now tell me what makes something a cult" would let you compare the
   same person's folk *prototype* against their folk *definition* — directly testing the
   prototype-theory framing the thesis already uses. More interviews would also help; 25 is thin.
8. **Track the argument over time.** The literature spans 2001–2024. Is the "cult" vs "new
   religious movement" dispute settling or entrenching? Since the field's biggest activity is
   that argument, its trajectory is a finding in itself.

**Bigger picture:**

9. **Test whether the folk concept fills the expert concept's blind spots.** Finding 5 suggests
   ordinary people's first thoughts land where the literature is vaguest. Systematising that — do
   interviews supply vocabulary literature lacks, or just vagueness? — would sharpen the
   folk-versus-expert argument into a claim about a real division of labour.
