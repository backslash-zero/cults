## 2. Recurring themes

**Charismatic/exploitative leader figure.** The single most consistent structural
element across the corpus, in both languages. English interviews name it directly —
b1-aug08-2128: *"there's a person who has another agenda and kind of manipulates
them"*; b1-aug09-2048: *"All those smaller church denominations with a charismatic
leader"*. French interviews converge on the same idea via "gourou": b3-aug18-1645
(*"Avec souvent un gourou"*), b3-aug21-1932 (*"qui se rassemble autour d'un
gourou"*), b3-aug22-2101 (*"une espèce de gourou... qui était au centre de
l'attention"*). This cross-linguistic convergence on a leader-centered definition is
a strong, well-evidenced pattern.

**Isolation and restricted freedom.** b1-aug08-2145 on the Children of God: *"very
isolated... very little contact is allowed outside of the group"*; b1-aug11-2011:
*"cult... also means a lack of freedom"*; b3-aug16-1517 on Menzil: *"They have a
whole town for themselves; getting in and out is controlled."*

**Financial exploitation.** b1-aug09-2048: *"extracts resources and sex from
them"*; b1-aug11-2011 ties cult membership to *"a financial commitment"*;
b3-aug22-2101: *"des histoires d'argent qui circulaient en faveur d'un... gourou"*
("money circulating in favor of a guru"). Notably used in the negative to argue
something is *not* a cult: b3-aug18-1645 on homeopathy — *"ça ne profite pas à une
personne"* ("it doesn't benefit one person") is given as the reason homeopathy
doesn't count.

**The cult/religion boundary is actively contested, not settled.** This is less a
single theme than a recurring argument interviewees have with themselves in real
time. b1-aug09-2048 stakes out the strongest version: *"every cult is a future
religion, unless it dies out before it has a chance to become a religion"* and
*"the difference between cult and religion... is as superficial as the difference
between dialects and languages."* b1-aug12-1231 arrives at a similar place from the
German-language angle: *"of course, all the established religions are also
cults."* Others resist collapsing the categories: b1-aug13-1342 argues *"sects
still fall under a higher umbrella of rules... whereas in a cult, you don't need
that."* b3-aug18-1645 spends most of the interview litigating specific
cases — are Jehovah's Witnesses a "secte" or a "religion"? — without resolving it:
*"Est-ce que c'est vraiment une secte? Est-ce que c'est pas une religion?"*

**"Cult" as adjective for devoted-but-harmless niche fandom.** A distinct,
lighter register shows up in b1-aug05-1650 (a tomato-growing hobby group
self-mockingly nicknamed "the cult") and b2-aug13-1832 (*"cult films... cult
bicycle parts"* as marketing-positive terms for small devoted followings, used
*"as a positive thing"*). b1-aug09-2124 (bike polo) sits in the same register. This
usage coexists in the same corpus as the harm-centered definitions above without
the interviewees treating them as contradictory — worth flagging for the
semantic-space study as evidence of "cult" spanning two barely-overlapping senses
in English.

**Documentary/media as the primary source of cult knowledge.** Several
interviewees explicitly source their mental model from Netflix/streaming
documentaries rather than direct experience: b1-aug08-2128 (*"Wild Wild
Country?"* — declined, but confirms documentary-sourced knowledge of an unnamed
land-purchase cult), b1-aug13-1342 (*"How to Become a Cult Leader"*, *"Holy
Hell"*), b2-aug13-1832 (looked up "the Moonies" mid-interview after a tangential
association). b1-aug08-1420 explicitly frames cult imagery as coming from *"media"*
and stereotyped along racial lines (*"usually white dudes"*), which the
interviewee then extends into an explicit critique connecting cult logic to
Zionism.

**Recurring named examples across the corpus:** Scientology (b1-aug08-2145,
b1-aug13-1342, b2-aug13-1840, b3-aug22-2057), Jehovah's Witnesses (b1-aug09-2048,
b1-aug11-2011, b3-aug18-1645), Raëlism (b1-aug13-1342, b3-aug18-1645,
b3-aug22-2101), Charles Manson/the Manson Family (b1-aug06-1955, b1-aug08-2145,
b3-aug16-1514), the Moonies/Unification Church (b2-aug13-1832), Hare Krishnas
(b1-aug12-1231), the Illuminati (b1-aug08-2137, b3-aug21-1932), Turkish tarikat
groups including Gülen/Menzil (b3-aug16-1517, ig-02).

## 3. Notable divergences and contradictions

- **Positive vs. harm-centered valence of "cult" itself.** b1-aug05-1650 and
  b2-aug13-1832 use "cult" affectionately/approvingly; nearly every other
  interview treats it as inherently negative or dangerous by default (e.g.
  b1-aug08-1420: *"not good"*; b2-aug13-1840: *"something like dark religious —
  like praying to the devil"*). No interviewee reconciles these two senses
  explicitly; they appear to be treated as unrelated word-senses rather than a
  single contested concept, which itself is a finding.
- **Is outward recruitment necessary to be a cult?** b1-aug11-2011 raises and
  partially resolves this: *"Can you be a cult without trying to get new people
  in? ... Probably, yes"* — but immediately re-ties the definition to "a lack of
  personal freedom... at a price," leaving the recruitment question unsettled
  rather than answered.
- **German lexical split not present in English or French.** b1-aug12-1231
  explicitly distinguishes German "Kult" (pop-cultural devotion, closer to the
  English *adjective* sense) from "Sekte" (a harmful closed group, state-registered
  church status being the implicit contrast) — *"if you say 'Kult,' you mean
  something that could also be more pop-cultural... but you would not say that
  about a sect."* Neither the English interviews nor the French interviews
  articulate this as a two-lexeme split — English collapses both senses into one
  word ("cult"), and French "secte" interviews (see below) lean toward the harmful,
  legally-inflected sense only. This is a concrete, well-evidenced cross-linguistic
  framing difference directly relevant to a semantic-space comparison.
- **French "secte" appears more narrowly/administratively scoped than English
  "cult."** b3-aug18-1645 asks *"est-ce qu'il y a des sectes actuellement
  certifiées?"* ("are there currently certified sects?") — implying "secte" carries
  a quasi-legal, officially-recognized-category connotation not present in how
  English interviewees use "cult" (freely applied to bike parts, tomato clubs,
  bike polo). None of the 5 French interviews use "secte" in the adjective/fandom
  sense that recurs in the English corpus (b1-aug05-1650, b1-aug09-2124,
  b2-aug13-1832) — this asymmetry is based on a small sample (n=5 French vs. n=21
  English) and should be treated as a hypothesis to check against a larger French
  sample, not a settled finding.
- **Visual/symbolic framing as an outlier.** ig-04 defines cult-association purely
  through a visual motif — *"People dressed in white"* — rather than through
  structure, leadership, or belief, unlike every other interview in the corpus.
  This is a single data point (n=1) but a striking one given how uniform the
  leader/isolation/money framing is elsewhere.

## 4. Vocabulary/framing patterns (semantic-space relevance)

- A tight recurring lexical cluster appears across both languages independent of
  translation: **leader/guru, manipulate/brainwash, isolate/control, closed
  group, money, extreme, belief/ideology.** The fact that the 5 French interviews
  converge on the same cluster (gourou, manipulation, groupe fermé, argent,
  idéologie) as the 21 English ones, without any cross-contamination (interviewer
  asked the same open-ended question in each interviewee's own language), is
  evidence of a fairly stable cross-linguistic "cult" concept core — useful as a
  positive control for the semantic-space study.
- The **adjective/noun split** is explicitly thematized by two interviewees in two
  different languages (b1-aug12-1231 in German, b2-aug13-1832 in English: *"whether
  it's adjective or noun... a certain devotion to something"*) — suggesting this
  distinction may be worth encoding as a separate dimension rather than folding it
  into a single "cult" embedding.
- "Privilege" as a frame for who gets to leave/choose a belief system is unique to
  b1-aug13-1342 (Syrian interviewee, living in Germany): *"when people are safe...
  they have the privilege to say 'I want to be out of this religious system.'"*
  This reframes cult-membership/non-membership as a function of material security
  rather than belief content — a framing absent from every other interview in the
  corpus and worth a closer read if the thesis explores non-Western or
  displacement-inflected framings of "cult."

## 5. Data quality and gaps

- **Two broken internal cross-references, left verbatim per instructions:**
  b1-aug05-1650 and b1-aug09-2124 both state nationality as "(see notes)" with no
  notes section present anywhere in the file.
- **Stale annotation in b2-aug13-1832:** its own TRANSCRIPTION/EDITING NOTES
  section references names ("Mr. Mooney," "Annie") that do not appear anywhere in
  the current dialogue text (which reads "Mr. Moon" and has no "Annie"),
  suggesting the notes predate the current revision of that file.
- **b3-aug16-1517 has no `INTERVIEW — [date], [time]` header**, unlike every other
  Batch 1–3 file; its date_time in the database is taken from the filename, not
  from in-file content.
- **ig-06 has fully redacted demographics** ("Demographics redacted. See ethical
  note") — usable for content analysis but not for any demographic
  cross-tabulation.
- **b3-aug23-1213's stated age (97) and gender are flagged as possibly
  unreliable/joking** by the batch's own prior editorial pass (`Transcripts
  Edited/README.txt`) — reported as-is in the database, but shouldn't be treated as
  a confirmed data point without caveats.
- **Modality confound: spoken vs. typed.** The Instagram Batch is DM-typed text,
  not a transcribed spoken interview — shorter, more fragmentary, sometimes sent as
  message bursts rather than continuous prose (see the ig-03 Q/A ratio outlier in
  Section 1). Any analysis that treats all 26 interviews as equivalent linguistic
  samples should account for this before drawing conclusions from raw word
  frequency or turn structure.
- **Two same-day Batch 3 pairs recorded minutes apart** (Aug 16, 15:14/15:17 —
  b3-aug16-1514/b3-aug16-1517; Aug 22, 20:57/21:01 — b3-aug22-2057/b3-aug22-2101)
  were already confirmed as distinct interviewees (different demographics) by the
  batch's own prior editorial pass, not duplicate recordings of the same person —
  noted here so it isn't re-investigated.
- **Sample size is small and uneven across batches** (11/2/7/6) and languages
  (21 English/5 French) — fine for qualitative theme-spotting as done above, but
  too small and too unevenly distributed for any quantitative claim about
  language- or nationality-based framing differences without treating them
  explicitly as hypotheses to test further, not conclusions.
